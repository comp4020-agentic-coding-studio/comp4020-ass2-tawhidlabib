148 studies, about 308,000 people, and a 50% greater likelihood of survival for
those with stronger social relationships. The comparison that made the paper
famous sits in the discussion: an effect comparable to giving up smoking, and
larger than obesity or physical inactivity. That sentence is why health
departments started using the word loneliness.

This lecture reads the paper properly, which means spending as long on what it
does not establish.

These are observational studies. People with more social connection differ from
people with less in many ways, and several of those ways — money, health at
baseline, mobility, mental illness — cause both the connection and the survival.
The arrow may also run backwards: being unwell shrinks your social world before
it kills you.

"Social relationship" is measured wildly differently across 148 studies, from
marital status to a count of contacts to a composite index. The meta-analysis
pools them, and the heterogeneity is not a footnote.

And the comparison to smoking is a comparison of effect sizes, not of
interventions. We know how to make someone stop smoking. Nobody knows how to
give someone a friend.

Cacioppo and Hawkley are the secondary reading and they supply the mechanism —
perceived isolation putting the brain into hypervigilance for social threat,
which then makes connection harder. That is the plausible pathway, and a
plausible pathway is not proof.

You should leave able to state the finding accurately and able to say what it
does not show. Both halves are marked.
