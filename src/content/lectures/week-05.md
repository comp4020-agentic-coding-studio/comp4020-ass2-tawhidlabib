A barbershop has a feature almost no other third place has: you cannot leave.
You are in the chair for forty minutes, facing a mirror, with a stranger's hands
on your head, and the social cost of silence is higher than the social cost of
talking. The room has arranged for a conversation to happen whether or not
either party wanted one.

Compare that to the counter in week 4. At the counter you can nurse a drink and
say nothing all evening. In the chair, not talking is a decision everybody
notices.

Anderson's market hall works differently again. His argument is that certain
settings act as a canopy — people practise civility across lines they would not
cross outside, watch each other doing it, and carry the practice out with them.
The room is not neutral by accident: stalls, shared seating and density of
observation all do work.

What connects the chair and the canopy is that both put strangers into
unavoidable proximity and then make it survivable. That is a different mechanism
from the one that produces regulars, and it is why this week is where the course
stops treating "third place" as one thing.

Read Anderson twice, once for the argument and once for the writing. He
describes for pages before he theorises, and your Field Notes are due this week.
