Everything so far has been a place people chose. The pub, the café, the chair,
the guild — someone decided to go. This week is about the rooms where nobody
decided anything.

A laundromat is a machine for producing forty idle minutes in the company of
strangers. A hospital waiting room produces more, under worse conditions. A bus
stop produces four. None of these is anybody's home away from home and none of
them wants to be.

Cattell and colleagues give us three verbs — mingling, observing, lingering —
and only the first is the sociable behaviour Oldenburg describes. Observing and lingering are what most people are doing in most public
space, and the paper finds that they carry benefit too. You do not have to talk
to anyone for a room to do something for you.

That is a serious problem for the eight rules, which put conversation at the
centre. If merely being among strangers, unspoken to, is doing part of the work,
then a great deal of ordinary public space has been undervalued, and the policy
question changes shape. It is much easier to protect a laundromat than to
manufacture a pub.

The ethics get harder here too, and this is where your week 3 position comes
back. The people in a hospital waiting room are having a bad day and have
consented to nothing. Thursday's brief is stricter than any other Round for that
reason, and the constraint is not bureaucratic — it is the subject matter.
